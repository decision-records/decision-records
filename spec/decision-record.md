# Decision Records (DRs) are short, lightweight documents used to capture significant choices, along with their context, rationale, and consequences. 

Their main goal is to preserve institutional knowledge, helping teams understand the underlying "why" behind a system's structure months or years after the choice was made. [1, 2, 3] 

## Decision Record Template

A high-quality decision record focuses on exactly one decision, usually spanning only a single page. The structure remains standard across industries and frameworks: [4, 5, 6, 7] 

* Title: A unique clear title that explains the purpose
* Status: The current lifecycle phase of the choice and dates (e.g., Proposed, Accepted, Deprecated, or Superseded).
* Context: The specific problem, constraints, or forces that prompted the decision.
* Options Considered: A summary of the alternative solutions evaluated, complete with trade-offs.
* Decision: The chosen path forward, written explicitly using an active voice.
* Consequences: The positive and negative outcomes resulting from the decision, including technical debt or future requirements. [3, 4, 6, 8, 9] 

## Core Benefits

* Prevents Regression: Stops teams from repeating expensive investigations or reverting to flawed solutions.
* Speeds Up Onboarding: Serves as a single source of truth for new team members to learn the history.
* Clarifies Thinking: The process of writing down trade-offs forces groups to think more deeply before executing.
 
## Implementation Best Practices

* Keep It Pithy: Records should be factual, concise, and focused strictly on decisions that are difficult to reverse. [4] 
* Store Near the Action: Keep decision files  directly within the relevant workspace. [3] 
* Avoid Log Fatigue: Do not document every tiny choice; restrict records to high-impact dependencies, system structures, and architecture-level requirements. [4, 10, 12, 13]
