# Enter the mid-market segment

* Status: request for comments
* Date: 2026-03-10
* Decision-maker(s): CEO, with Board input (to be confirmed)
* Owner: VP Strategy

Summary: Work in progress. This is a starting point for a team that is considering whether and how to extend a go-to-market approach built for large customers to a mid-sized customer segment. It shows how a team can frame the question, debate the options, and gather information. It records no decision.

## Issue

A company that sells to large enterprise customers sees its pipeline flattening and its win rate staying flat, while inbound interest from smaller companies is growing. Today it either turns this demand away or serves it poorly with a sales approach designed for large deals. The board wants a credible growth path beyond saturation of the enterprise segment.

The questions the team must answer:

* Is the mid-market segment attractive for us (size, growth, needs we can meet, competition)?
* Can we win and serve it profitably with our product, price, and sales approach, or does something need to change?
* How should we enter, and with what resources, given that new hiring may not be possible?
* What would tell us early that it is working or not?

## Assumptions

Each assumption is something to test.

* **Mid-market buyers decide faster but spend less per customer.** Test: interview recent mid-sized prospects and look at deal length and size in the data we already have.
* **The product needs modest simplification to suit mid-market onboarding.** Test: watch mid-sized customers use it, and list the steps that cause confusion.
* **Existing sales talent can adapt to a faster, lighter-touch approach.** Test: ask the sales team what they would need, and look at who has done this before.
* **Entering the segment will not harm enterprise results.** Test: estimate how much enterprise capacity the move would take, and from which teams.

## Constraints

* No new hiring may be approved this year; any move must be funded from existing budget.
* Enterprise revenue commitments for the current year must not be put at risk.
* Contract terms built for large buyers may be too heavy for faster deals; Legal has flagged this.
* Unknown, to be confirmed: the size of the segment in our territories, pricing the segment will accept, and support load.

## Options considered

For each option, estimate value, costs, timeline, and capability needs, and note what evidence would change the team's mind.

* **Stay enterprise-only and invest further in enterprise expansion.**
  * Value: lowest execution risk; builds on what works.
  * Risks: does not address the flat pipeline; skeptics doubt it reaches growth targets alone.
* **Enter with a dedicated new team.**
  * Value: cleanest execution and focus.
  * Cost drivers: recruiting, onboarding, salaries, management time; blocked if hiring is frozen.
  * Timeline shape: slow to start because of hiring; long ramp.
* **Enter by reallocating part of existing enterprise sales capacity**, with a simplified onboarding process.
  * Value: fundable now; reversible if it underperforms; real data on mid-market economics before a larger commitment.
  * Cost drivers: lost enterprise coverage; retraining; product and legal work for simpler onboarding and terms.
  * Capabilities needed: a pod with clear leadership; product changes; simplified contract terms.
  * Risks: slower ramp than a dedicated team; enterprise pipeline suffers.
* **Enter through channel or reseller partners.**
  * Value: lowest internal cost; reach.
  * Cost drivers: partner margin, enablement, partner management.
  * Risks: weaker control of the customer relationship; results may lag direct sales (check with peer companies).
* **Serve the segment with a self-serve product** and no sales involvement. Test willingness to buy without contact.

## Argument

Points of debate the team should expect:

* **Growth versus focus.** Does adding a segment dilute what makes the company good at enterprise?
* **Speed versus reversibility.** Which options can be undone cheaply if the assumptions prove wrong?
* **Who pays for it?** Reallocation protects the budget but moves cost into lost enterprise attention.
* **Position.** Using the Ansoff matrix, selling an existing product to a new market (market development) is less risky than also changing the product (diversification). Where does this move sit?

How the team could evaluate costs and trade-offs:

1. Size the opportunity as a range: number of target firms, share reachable, expected deal value, win rate, and sales cycle, with clearly labelled assumptions.
2. Cost the entry: capacity moved, product changes, legal work, marketing, partner terms, and the opportunity cost of what enterprise sales would otherwise do.
3. Compare options under best, likely, and worst scenarios; compare payback period and learning value.
4. Choose leading indicators in advance: qualified opportunities, conversion at each stage, time to close, and cost to acquire a customer, along with effects on enterprise pipeline.
5. Use a weighted matrix (value, cost, speed, reversibility, risk to the core business) and a pre-mortem.

## Decision

**Not yet decided.** This is a starting point. A team using it should replace this section with its own decision.

Suggested way to reach a decision:

* Confirm who decides, and what the board needs to see.
* Agree criteria and weights first.
* Gather the evidence above and record what was found.
* Share for comment, then decide and update the Status.

## Implications

If the team chooses a staged entry, it would need to:

* Name which capacity moves, and when, and what happens to enterprise targets.
* Plan product, legal, and onboarding work that the segment needs.
* Agree checkpoints (for example at the end of two periods) with clear criteria for continuing, expanding, or stopping.
* Report segment results to the board in a consistent way.
