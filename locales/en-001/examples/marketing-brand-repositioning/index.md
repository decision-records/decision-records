# Reposition the brand around reliability

* Status: request for comments
* Date: 2026-01-15
* Decision-maker(s): CMO (to be confirmed)
* Owner: Head of Brand

Summary: Work in progress. This is a starting point for a team that is considering whether to change the core message of its brand, for example from "innovative" to "reliable". It shows how a team can frame the question, debate the options, and gather information. It records no decision.

## Issue

Interviews with customers who won and lost deals suggest that customers often choose the company over faster-moving competitors because of its track record and support, and not because they see it as the most innovative, which is the current brand message. If that is true, the messaging may be working against the reasons customers buy. The team must decide whether to change it, how far, and how.

The questions to answer:

* What do customers and prospects actually value, and how strong is the evidence?
* How is the brand perceived today, and by whom?
* If we change the message, what changes with it (visuals, sales material, product claims), and what must stay the same?
* How would we know that the change helped?

## Assumptions

Each assumption is something to test.

* **The reliability advantage is durable**, not a temporary effect of a competitor's recent problem. Test: look at the trend over a longer period and in several segments.
* **Existing messaging is not so entrenched that a change would confuse customers.** Test: ask customers and the sales team how they describe the company now.
* **Sales and Customer Success can express the new message consistently.** Test: run a small pilot with a group of them and compare.
* **Win and loss interviews are representative.** Test: check sample size, who was interviewed, and who is missing.

## Constraints

* No budget for a full visual rebrand; any change must work within the existing visual system.
* Claims already made in signed contracts must not be contradicted.
* Any reliability claim (such as availability statistics) must be independently verifiable before publication.
* Unknown, to be confirmed: how competitors are positioned, and how much the message appears in product, support, and recruiting.

## Options considered

For each option, estimate value, costs, timeline, and capability needs, and note what evidence would change the team's mind.

* **Keep "innovative" and add proof points.**
  * Value: no repositioning risk.
  * Risks: may optimise a message customers do not buy for.
* **Reposition fully around reliability, in phases** (for example sales material and paid channels first, wider refresh later).
  * Value: aligns the message with reasons for purchase; phasing limits risk and lets the team learn.
  * Cost drivers: creative work; legal verification of claims; training; updates across channels.
* **Run both messages in parallel by channel.**
  * Value: avoids full commitment.
  * Risks: mixed messages may weaken recall; test with data before relying on it.
* **Position around a combination** (reliability with a secondary differentiator) so the brand does not read as dull next to a competitor who stresses speed.
* **Do not change the message yet** and invest first in better research on customer value.

A useful way to structure the debate is the positioning approach described by April Dunford: competitive alternatives, unique attributes, the value they enable, the customers who care most, and the market category that makes the value obvious.

## Argument

Points of debate the team should expect:

* **Evidence versus instinct.** How much weight do interviews deserve against sales and leadership instincts?
* **Reliable or dull?** Sales may worry that "reliable" sounds unexciting against a competitor. Can it be paired with something that gives energy?
* **Depth of change.** Message only, or product, support, and hiring too? A message that the company cannot back up damages trust.
* **Speed.** Faster change gets benefits sooner; slower change allows learning.

How the team could evaluate costs and trade-offs:

1. Define the outcomes expected: win rate in target segments, consideration, sales cycle, brand perception, retention, and referral.
2. Cost the options: research, creative, channel updates, legal review, training, and the cost of mixed messages during transition. Show ranges.
3. Test before committing: message tests with customers, small campaign tests, and a pilot with part of the sales team.
4. Weigh options on value, cost, risk, reversibility, and fit with existing contracts.
5. Run a pre-mortem and name the leading indicators that would trigger a pause.

## Decision

**Not yet decided.** This is a starting point. A team using it should replace this section with its own decision.

Suggested way to reach a decision:

* Confirm who decides and who must agree (Sales, Legal, Product).
* Agree criteria and weights first.
* Gather the evidence above and record what was found.
* Share for comment, then decide and update the Status.

## Implications

If the team decides to reposition in phases, it would need to:

* Update sales material and paid channel creative first, and agree how to measure results.
* Have Legal validate every claim before release.
* Give Sales and Customer Success talking points and training.
* Plan a follow-on decision on any wider visual refresh once early results are in.
* Confirm existing signed contracts are unaffected, since the change would be forward-looking.
