# Lean manufacturing

* Status: request for comments
* Date: 2025-11-24
* Decision-maker(s): Plant Director (to be confirmed)
* Owner: Head of Operational Excellence

Summary: Work in progress. This is a starting point for a team that is considering how to introduce lean manufacturing, which focuses on removing waste and delivering what the customer values, in a plant that has several production lines. It shows how a team can frame the question, debate options, and gather information. It records no decision.

## Issue

A plant that makes custom products on several production lines has lead times longer than customers expect, work-in-progress stock that fills much of the floor, and operators who report a lot of waiting, rework, and movement of materials. Leaders are interested in lean manufacturing, but a change across the whole plant at once could disrupt orders that must be delivered this year.

The questions to answer:

* Where does time actually go between an order arriving and delivery, and how much of it adds value for the customer?
* Is lean the right response, and which parts (flow, pull, standard work, problem solving) would help most?
* Where do we start, how do we involve operators, and when do we extend?
* What would show that it is working?

## Assumptions

Each assumption is something to test.

* **Waiting, rework, and movement are the main causes of long lead times.** Test: walk the process (go to the "gemba", the place where work happens) and map the flow of one order from start to finish.
* **One line is representative of the others.** Test: compare lines on product mix, repeat work, equipment, and staffing.
* **Operators will propose and test improvements if given time and a role.** Test: ask operators what they would change and what stops them today.
* **Lead time is a fair measure of progress.** Test: agree how lead time, work-in-progress, rework, and on-time delivery will be measured before changing anything.

## Constraints

* Orders already promised must be delivered; any change must protect them.
* Planning must cope with some lines working one way and some another.
* Operators need protected time for improvement work.
* Unknown, to be confirmed: equipment constraints, maintenance windows, supplier lead times, and the effect of customer demand swings.

## Options considered

For each option, estimate value, costs, timeline, and capability needs, and write down the evidence that would change the team's mind.

* **Change every line at once.**
  * Value: fastest route to a consistent plant.
  * Cost drivers: training; changeover; temporary loss of output; management attention across all lines.
  * Risks: threatens delivery of current orders; operators have no time to learn and adjust.
* **Adopt line by line, starting with a pilot line.** Map the value stream for one line, reduce waste there, and use the results to choose practices for the rest.
  * Value: contains disruption; operators see real results before their own line changes; learning carries forward.
  * Cost drivers: coaching; operator time for improvement work; running lines in different ways for a time.
  * Timeline shape: sequential, with each line starting only after the previous one has held its improvement for a stated period.
  * Capabilities needed: someone who can lead value-stream mapping; reliable measures; sponsor support.
  * Risks: slow; the pilot line may not be typical.
* **Introduce lean tools plant-wide without redesigning the lines** (for example 5S: sort, set in order, shine, standardise, sustain).
  * Value: visible and inexpensive.
  * Risks: improves tidiness without changing flow, so lead times may change little.
* **Combine lean with other methods** such as Six Sigma where variation is also a problem.
* **Keep the current approach** and fix specific bottlenecks. A real baseline to cost honestly.

## Argument

Points of debate the team should expect:

* **Contain risk or move fast?** A pilot is safer but slower. How much is speed worth against the risk to current orders?
* **Tools versus system.** Lean is a way of thinking about value and flow, not a collection of tools. How much culture change is the team prepared to lead?
* **Which line first?** The one with the most repeat work (clearest results), the one with the worst problems (most to gain), or the one with the most willing team (most likely to succeed)?
* **What do operators get?** If improvement only means more output, expect little engagement.

How the team could evaluate costs and trade-offs:

1. Map the current state and estimate where lead time goes; cost waiting, rework, and inventory in the organisation's own terms.
2. Estimate programme costs: coaching, operator time, training, any equipment or layout changes, and the cost of lost output during change. Give ranges.
3. Choose measures first: lead time, work-in-progress, rework, on-time delivery, and some measure of operator engagement or safety. A reference for definitions of manufacturing indicators is ISO 22400-2.
4. Compare options under best, likely, and worst cases, and test how sensitive the answer is to the assumptions the team is least sure about.
5. Use a weighted decision matrix and a pre-mortem.

## Decision

**Not yet decided.** This is a starting point. A team using it should replace this section with its own decision.

Suggested way to reach a decision:

* Confirm who decides and by when.
* Agree criteria and weights first.
* Gather the evidence above and record what was found.
* Share for comment, then decide and update the Status.

## Implications

If the team chooses a line-by-line approach, it would need to:

* Name a leader for the pilot, publish the value-stream map and results, and set a lead-time target for the pilot line.
* Fund operators' time for proposing and testing improvements.
* Agree what "held" means before starting the next line.
* Plan around lines that work in different ways during the rollout.
* Say openly which line is next and under what conditions.
