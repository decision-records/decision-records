# Target operating model

* Status: request for comments
* Date: 2026-09-15
* Decision-maker(s): CEO and executive team (to be confirmed)
* Owner: Chief Operating Officer

Summary: Work in progress. This is a starting point for a team that is considering how to describe and move toward a target operating model: how the organization will deliver its strategy through processes, people, technology, locations, and governance. It shows how a team can frame the question, debate options, and gather information. It records no decision.

## Issue

An organization has grown by acquisition and by hiring, and its structure now reflects that history rather than a plan. Business units run their own finance, support, and procurement; customers meet different teams, systems, and prices depending on which unit they first contacted; leaders cannot say who is accountable for end-to-end outcomes such as onboarding a customer.

The questions to answer:

* What must the organization be able to do to deliver its strategy, and where does the current structure prevent it?
* What are the realistic ways to organize, and what does each give up?
* Which parts should be shared and which should stay close to customers?
* How would the organization move there in stages, and how would people be treated during the change?

## Assumptions

Each assumption is something to test.

* **Duplicated functions are a real cost, not just an irritation.** Test: map one function across units and compare the effort and results.
* **Customers notice the inconsistency.** Test: trace customer journeys across units; look at complaints and effort.
* **Shared services can meet the needs of teams that depend on them.** Test: ask the dependent teams what service levels they would need.
* **Delivery should stay close to customers.** Test: ask leaders of large units what would be lost if delivery were centralized.

## Constraints

* The target must be described in a single document covering processes, roles, technology, locations, and governance.
* The change must be staged, and the order of moves is a separate decision.
* People affected need clear, fair treatment, and no one should learn of an individual outcome before it is decided; take advice on employment law.
* Unknown, to be confirmed: which systems would need to be unified, and contract and regulatory constraints.

## Options considered

For each option, estimate value, costs, timeline, and capability needs, and note what evidence would change the team's mind.

* **Keep the business-unit model and harmonize at the edges.**
  * Value: lowest disruption; keeps local autonomy.
  * Risks: leaves duplicated functions and an inconsistent customer experience.
* **Move to a functional model** in which sales, delivery, finance, and support each become company-wide functions.
  * Value: removes duplication; clear functional standards.
  * Risks: separates teams from the customers and products they serve; delivery speed may suffer.
* **Hybrid: shared services plus customer-aligned teams.** Finance, people, procurement, and support become shared services; sales and delivery stay aligned to customer segments.
  * Value: much of the efficiency of the functional model while keeping delivery close to customers.
  * Cost drivers: design effort; service-level agreements; systems; transition costs; management time.
  * Risks: the most complex to design and run.
* **Different models for different parts of the business**, chosen deliberately.

## Argument

Points of debate the team should expect:

* **Autonomy versus consistency.** How much local freedom is worth the duplication?
* **Where does the customer meet us?** Organizing around customers and organizing around functions pull in different directions (Conway's law notes that organizations tend to produce designs that mirror their communication structures).
* **Service levels.** Shared services work only if the teams that depend on them trust the commitments.
* **Pace.** A staged approach lowers risk but prolongs the period of mixed models.

How the team could evaluate costs and trade-offs:

1. Define design principles and desired outcomes first, such as onboarding time, cost of shared services, customer satisfaction by segment, and accountability for end-to-end results.
2. Estimate transition costs and ongoing costs of each model in the organization's terms: change effort, systems, redundancy or reassignment, management time, and temporary loss of output. Show ranges.
3. Test each model against realistic customer journeys and incidents.
4. Compare options on value, cost, risk, speed, and people impact, using a weighted matrix.
5. Agree a review point against the outcome measures set in the document.

## Decision

**Not yet decided.** This is a starting point. A team using it should replace this section with its own decision.

Suggested way to reach a decision:

* Confirm who decides, and who must be consulted.
* Agree design principles and criteria first.
* Gather the evidence above and record what was found.
* Share for comment, then decide and update the Status.

## Implications

If the team decides to move to a hybrid model, it would need to:

* Name an owner for the target operating model document and a date for its first version.
* Publish service levels and named owners for each shared service before any team is moved onto it.
* Decide separately how role changes are handled.
* Review the model against the outcome measures after a stated period.
