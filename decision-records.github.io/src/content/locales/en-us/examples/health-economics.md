# Weigh health economics in a treatment funding decision

* Status: request for comments
* Date: 2026-05-12
* Decision-maker(s): Regional commissioning board (to be confirmed)
* Owner: Director of Strategic Commissioning

Summary: Work in progress. This is a starting point for a health commissioning team that is considering whether and how to fund a new treatment, weighing clinical benefit, cost-effectiveness, budget impact, and equity. It shows how a team can frame the question, debate options, and gather information. It records no decision.

## Issue

A new medicine for a long-term condition that affects many people in a region has been proposed for funding. Trial evidence suggests an improvement in quality of life and a reduction in hospital admissions. The budget is fixed, so funding the medicine means funding something else less. A decision based only on price, or only on clinical benefit, ignores that trade-off.

The questions to answer:

* How large and how certain is the benefit, for which patients?
* What does it cost, and what is the cost per unit of health gained compared with other uses of the same money?
* What is the budget impact, over several years, and what would be displaced?
* How does each option affect equity and health inequalities?

## Assumptions

Each assumption is something to test.

* **Trial results apply to our patients.** Test: compare trial populations with local patients, and consider real-world evidence.
* **The group likely to gain most can be identified reliably.** Test: define criteria and check against local records.
* **The supplier will agree to terms such as a lower price and data collection.** Test: open discussions early.
* **Outcome data can actually be collected with local systems.** Test: check what is recorded and what would need new capture.

## Constraints

* The budget is fixed.
* The cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained should be considered against the thresholds and practice used by the decision-making body.
* Any managed arrangement needs data that can be collected and reviewed.
* Unknown, to be confirmed: the number of patients, the price and its confidentiality, other treatments, and any national guidance that applies.

## Options considered

For each option, estimate value, costs, timeline, and capability needs, and note what evidence would change the team's mind.

* **Fund for all eligible patients.**
  * Value: access for all who are eligible; simple to run.
  * Risks: the budget impact may require cuts elsewhere; cost per QALY may exceed accepted levels.
* **Do not fund.**
  * Value: no budget impact; no displacement.
  * Risks: patients lose a treatment with a demonstrated benefit; inequalities may widen for those without other options.
* **Fund for a defined group under a managed-access agreement.** For example those who benefit most, with the supplier adjusting the price, collecting outcome data, and a review after a stated period.
  * Value: moves cost per QALY toward accepted levels; limits budget impact.
  * Cost drivers: negotiation; monitoring; data collection; administration.
  * Risks: excludes some patients; depends on supplier agreement and on data that can be collected.
* **Defer** while more evidence is gathered, accepting that patients wait.
* **Fund on a time-limited basis** with a pre-agreed review.

## Argument

Points of debate the team should expect:

* **Efficiency versus equity.** A treatment may be cost-effective overall yet benefit groups that are already advantaged more.
* **Evidence and uncertainty.** How should the board treat long-term benefit estimated from short trials?
* **Thresholds.** Where should the line be and how flexible? The QALY (see the MIDAS explainer) is widely used but also criticized; the team should know its limits.
* **Transparency.** What will be published about the reasons?

How the team could evaluate costs and trade-offs:

1. Use established methods of health technology assessment: cost-effectiveness or cost-utility analysis expressed per QALY gained, with uncertainty shown through sensitivity analysis (see NICE's methods manual for a worked approach).
2. Estimate budget impact over several years under different uptake scenarios, and name what would be displaced.
3. Consider equity: who gains, who is excluded, and the effect on health inequalities.
4. Decide what evidence a managed-access agreement would collect, and what result would change the decision at review.
5. Document the rationale so that it can be explained and challenged.

## Decision

**Not yet decided.** This is a starting point. A team using it should replace this section with its own decision.

Suggested way to reach a decision:

* Confirm who decides and under what rules.
* Agree criteria and weights first, including how equity counts.
* Gather the evidence above and record what was found.
* Share for comment where appropriate, then decide and update the Status.

## Implications

If the team decides on a managed-access arrangement, it would need to:

* Negotiate the terms; if the supplier will not agree, bring the matter back to the board.
* Name what is displaced, rather than leaving it unstated.
* Publish eligibility criteria and the reasons for them.
* Measure outcomes, including uptake by group, and review equity at the review point.
* State openly the uncertainty in the evidence.
