# Business frameworks

* Status: request for comments
* Date: 2026-10-01
* Decision-maker(s): CEO and executive team (to be confirmed)
* Owner: Chief of Staff

Summary: Work in progress. This is a starting point for a team that is considering how to introduce a company-wide goal-setting framework such as Objectives and Key Results (OKRs). It shows how a team can frame the question, debate the options, and gather evidence. It does not record a decision.

## Issue

Leadership is considering a common goal-setting framework, because teams currently set goals in different formats and at different rhythms, and leaders cannot easily see how team work connects to company priorities. Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) is the leading candidate, but the team has not yet agreed that OKRs are the right framework at all. A past attempt to roll out a new planning process everywhere at once stalled: managers were not trained, the tooling was unsettled, and people treated it as extra reporting.

The questions the team needs to answer:

* Is a shared framework the right response to the visibility problem, or is something simpler enough?
* If yes, is OKRs the right framework, or would another approach (for example a balanced scorecard, or management by objectives) fit better?
* How should the framework be introduced: all at once, in phases, or only at the top?
* What would make a phase "done", so the next one can start?

## Assumptions

Each assumption is something to test, not something to take for granted.

* **Teams will use the framework if it saves them effort.** Test: interview a few teams about how they set and track goals today and what they would give up.
* **A small pilot is representative.** Test: choose pilot teams that differ in kind (for example one customer-facing, one product, one support) and check whether their experience diverges.
* **Leaders will make time for training.** Test: ask leaders what they would stop doing to make room, and check calendars before committing.
* **The visibility problem is caused by inconsistent goals, not by something else** such as unclear priorities or weak reporting. Test: trace two or three company priorities down to team work and see where the link breaks.

## Constraints

* A previous rollout stalled, so patience for a repeat is limited. Confirm what exactly went wrong last time.
* Managers need to be trained before their teams are asked to use the framework.
* Tooling choice should follow what the pilot teams actually need, but some interim way of recording goals is needed from the start.
* Unknown, to be confirmed: how the framework relates to existing planning, budgeting, and performance cycles, and what leadership will and will not link to it.

## Options considered

For each option the team should estimate value, cost, timeline, and capability needs, and write down what evidence would change its mind.

* **Introduce everywhere at the same time.**
  * Value: one shared language quickly; no period of two systems side by side.
  * Cost drivers: training for every manager at once; time for every team to write and review goals; support load on whoever owns the framework; the cost of a failed rollout (lost credibility).
  * Timeline shape: short to start, long to stabilize. Problems are found by everyone simultaneously.
  * Capabilities needed: trained facilitators at scale; settled tooling.
  * Risks: repeats the conditions of the earlier stall.
* **Introduce in stages: leadership team, then a few pilot departments, then everyone.**
  * Value: each phase teaches the team what to change before the next; early examples persuade sceptics.
  * Cost drivers: a longer period of running two goal-setting approaches; extra coordination; extra training and review time for the pilot teams, which should be budgeted explicitly.
  * Timeline shape: sequential phases, each lasting long enough to complete at least one full goal cycle including scoring and review.
  * Capabilities needed: a named owner; written exit criteria for each phase; a way to gather pilot feedback.
  * Risks: momentum fades between phases; pilot teams are not representative.
* **Adopt only at the leadership level.**
  * Value: lowest effort and risk.
  * Cost drivers: small, but the benefit may be small too.
  * Risks: does not solve visibility below the leadership team.
* **Do nothing new, and instead fix the visibility problem another way** (for example a simple shared priorities document). Worth costing honestly as the baseline against which the other options are judged.

## Argument

Points of debate the team should expect:

* **Speed versus learning.** Moving everywhere at once is faster but gives no chance to fix the framework before everyone depends on it. How much is the speed worth?
* **Fit versus fashion.** OKRs are widely discussed, but the team should ask what problem they solve here and what a good outcome looks like in terms of observable change.
* **Cost of two systems.** Phasing means some teams work in the old way for a while. Is that acceptable, and for how long?

How the team could evaluate costs and trade-offs:

1. List one-time costs (design, training, facilitation, tooling set-up) and recurring costs (time spent writing, reviewing, and scoring goals; tool licences; owner time) in the organisation's own terms, such as person-time and spend, as ranges rather than single numbers.
2. List benefits the team can observe: fewer duplicated or conflicting goals, faster alignment conversations, better ability to trace work to priorities.
3. Compare options under at least three scenarios (works well, works partly, stalls) and ask which option is least regretted if it stalls.
4. Use a simple weighted decision matrix for criteria such as learning, speed, risk, cost, and fit with existing planning.
5. Run a pre-mortem: assume the rollout failed in a year and list the reasons.

## Decision

**Not yet decided.** This record is a starting point. A team using it should replace this section with its own decision once the questions above are answered.

Suggested way to reach a decision:

* Confirm who decides (a person, or a rule such as consensus with escalation after a stated period).
* Agree the criteria and their weights before comparing options.
* Gather the evidence listed under Assumptions, and record what was found.
* Share the record for comment with a deadline, then decide and update the Status.

## Implications

If the team does choose a phased approach, it would need to:

* Name an owner and publish exit criteria for every phase (for example leaders trained, a full cycle scored, pilot teams report the process is worth the time).
* Budget time for training and review explicitly, rather than absorbing it silently.
* Decide tooling late, using what the pilot teams needed.
* Agree what happens if a phase misses its criteria: repeat it, adjust the framework, or stop.
* Decide separately, and later, whether to link the framework to pay or performance reviews.
