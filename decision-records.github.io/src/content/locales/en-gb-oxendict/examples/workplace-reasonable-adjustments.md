# Workplace reasonable adjustments

* Status: request for comments
* Date: 2026-03-30
* Decision-maker(s): Chief People Officer (to be confirmed)
* Owner: Head of Employee Experience

Summary: Work in progress. This is a starting point for a team that is designing a clear, fast, private, and fair process for workplace adjustments for disabled employees and candidates. It shows how a team can frame the question, debate options, and gather information. It records no decision and is not legal advice.

## Issue

In many places the law requires employers to make reasonable adjustments (called reasonable accommodations in some countries) so that a disabled employee or candidate is not at a disadvantage, and it is also simply the right way to treat people. Requests are currently handled differently by each manager: some people wait a long time, some are asked to explain their condition repeatedly, and some are refused with no reason given. Managers say they do not know what is reasonable or who pays.

The questions to answer:

* What do the laws in the places where we operate require, and what do good practice and our values ask beyond that?
* What do employees and candidates experience now, and what would they want?
* Who decides, who pays, how fast, and how is privacy protected?
* How do we handle a refusal, an appeal, and a change in circumstances?

## Assumptions

Each assumption is something to test.

* **Inconsistent handling is a real problem.** Test: review recent requests (without identifying individuals) for time taken, outcome, and reasons given.
* **People can describe what they need without disclosing a diagnosis.** Test: ask employees and advisers, and look at how guidance from bodies such as Acas or the Job Accommodation Network frames requests.
* **Managers can decide well with training and an adviser.** Test: talk with managers about what they would need.
* **A central budget improves fairness.** Test: check how costs are paid today and whether cost has influenced outcomes.

## Constraints

* Legal requirements differ by country and region; take local legal advice. This record is not legal advice.
* Refusals must be given in writing with the reason and a route to appeal.
* Managers should not be told a diagnosis unless the person chooses to share it.
* Unknown, to be confirmed: volume and cost of requests, the occupational health or adviser capacity, and relevant collective agreements.

## Options considered

For each option, estimate value, costs, timeline, and capability needs, and note what evidence would change the team's mind.

* **Leave it to line managers.**
  * Value: decisions close to the person and the job.
  * Risks: inconsistent results; managers untrained for the decision; medical detail exposed to more people than necessary.
* **Send every request to a central team.**
  * Value: consistency and privacy.
  * Risks: slow for simple requests; the team may not understand the role.
* **A central team sets the process and pays; managers decide on advice.** The person describes what they need, not their diagnosis; the manager and the person agree an adjustment, using a trained adviser where needed, with a central budget.
  * Value: fast for simple requests, consistent, and fair on cost.
  * Cost drivers: the budget; the adviser; training; administration.
* **Tiered process.** Simple requests are agreed locally and quickly; complex ones go to an adviser.
* **Use an external provider** for assessments and advice.

## Argument

Points of debate the team should expect:

* **Speed versus consistency.** How can simple requests be decided fast without losing fairness?
* **Privacy.** What does a manager need to know, and what must stay confidential?
* **What is reasonable.** Legal tests (for example in the UK Equality Act 2010, or the US Americans with Disabilities Act) consider factors such as effectiveness, cost, and practicability. How will the organization apply them consistently?
* **Cost.** Who pays, and how does the organization avoid cost deciding the outcome?

How the team could evaluate costs and trade-offs:

1. Estimate the volume and type of requests from past data, and the likely cost range, including one-off and recurring adjustments.
2. Estimate the cost of the process itself: a central budget, adviser time, training, and administration.
3. Count the costs of not acting well: time lost, attrition, legal exposure, and harm to people.
4. Compare options on speed, consistency, privacy, fairness, cost, and legal risk, and involve disabled employees in the weighting.
5. Choose measures such as time to agreement, satisfaction, number of refusals and appeals, and cost, reported in aggregate without identifying individuals.

## Decision

**Not yet decided.** This is a starting point. A team using it should replace this section with its own decision.

Suggested way to reach a decision:

* Confirm who decides and who is consulted, including employee representatives.
* Agree criteria and weights first.
* Gather the evidence above and record what was found.
* Share for comment, then decide and update the Status.

## Implications

If the team decides on a central budget with decisions between the person and their manager, it would need to:

* Publish the process, form, expected timelines, and a guide for managers.
* Train managers before they handle requests.
* Set up the budget and an adviser.
* Review adjustments when a person's role or health changes.
* Report requests, timing, and cost each year without identifying individuals.
